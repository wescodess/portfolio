# Senior engineer portfolio reference research

Research date: 2026-09-05  
Audience: hiring managers and senior engineering peers evaluating Wesley Ukadike  
Decision context: a senior Nuxt/Vue/full-stack product engineer; lead with Budds, CharterXE, and WACS; no personal photography; one restrained Bitmoji is acceptable; avoid the interchangeable “AI portfolio template” look.

## Method

This is an audit of 65 personal sites belonging to engineers, design engineers, technical founders, maintainers, or web practitioners whose own sites show a senior title, leadership role, foundational body of work, or long public track record. The portfolio itself is the primary source in every entry. I checked that each URL resolved on 2026-09-05 and inspected its homepage structure and visible content. A title is only reported when the site states it; otherwise the evidence column identifies the public work or tenure that supports inclusion.

This is design research, not a ranking of people. Some references are excellent examples of a single quality but poor templates for Wesley as a whole. “Caution” records that boundary.

## Repeated patterns worth adopting

### 1. Seniority is usually inferred from evidence, not announced

The strongest sites rarely lead with “I am a passionate senior engineer.” They lead with a current role and specific body of work: “co-founder of…,” “creator of…,” “leading the Nuxt core team,” “I make Polypane,” or “I work on the Web team at Linear.” That phrasing is short because the linked evidence carries the claim.

For Wesley, the opening should do the same:

> Wesley Ukadike is a senior product engineer in Toronto. He builds web products across AI media, aviation, and operational systems.

Then show Budds immediately. Do not add an invented maxim beneath it.

### 2. The credible default is an edited index

Mitchell Hashimoto, Brian Lovin, Emil Kowalski, Paco Coursey, Anthony Fu, Daniel Roe, Eric Bailey, and Max Böck all use variants of the same underlying structure:

1. Who I am / what I do now
2. A deliberately small set of work or current artifacts
3. Writing, talks, or other public evidence
4. Contact

They differ in voice, typography, and artifacts, but they do not pretend the homepage is a product dashboard or cinematic brand campaign.

### 3. Real artifacts provide the visual identity

The least generic sites do not decorate every section with an unrelated visual system. They use the work itself: book covers, product screenshots, demos, diagrams, article illustrations, or open-source project names. Wesley already has the necessary color and specificity in Budds, CharterXE, and WACS. The portfolio shell can therefore be quiet.

### 4. Recency is a trust signal

Current writing, a “Now” note, recent releases, talks, streams, or a current project make a site feel maintained. Wesley does not need a blog feed to get this benefit. A small, dated “Currently” line—only when true—or a case-study “Last updated” note is enough.

### 5. Personality comes from authored constraints

The memorable sites are personal because of consistent choices: Julia Evans’s drawings, Jim Nielsen’s microcopy, Hidde de Vries’s operating statements, Anthony Fu’s project ecology, Mitchell Hashimoto’s candor, or Max Böck’s ordinary opening. Personality is not synonymous with maximalism. Wesley’s one restrained Bitmoji can work if the rest of the site sounds like him.

### 6. Deep pages and shallow homepages serve different jobs

Senior work often cannot be compressed responsibly into a card. The homepage should help a visitor choose a project; a detail page should explain context, ownership, constraints, decisions, collaboration, and outcome. Addy Osmani’s “Built” format is especially useful for the detail-page content model, even though its visual treatment should not be copied.

## Repeated anti-patterns

- Giant manifesto headlines that could belong to any engineer.
- Abstract section names that force the visitor to learn a metaphor before seeing the work.
- Numbered `01 / 02 / 03` navigation used only as decoration.
- Dark gradient backgrounds, glowing blobs, glass cards, terminal chrome, faux operating systems, and command palettes with no product purpose.
- Technology pills or logo walls standing in for demonstrated engineering decisions.
- Equal-weight project grids that make a production system and a weekend experiment look equivalent.
- “Impact” numbers without source, scope, baseline, or ownership.
- Case studies that describe the product but never say what the author personally owned.
- Excessive scroll choreography that makes hiring readers wait for evidence.
- Generic values such as “craft,” “innovation,” “scalability,” and “user-centricity” without a linked decision.
- Social-proof walls, badges, and testimonial carousels that make an individual engineer look like a marketing funnel.
- Copying the surface style of a well-known designer’s site without the body of work that made the style credible.
